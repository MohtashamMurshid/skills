# Narrative blueprints

Choose the closest sequence and adapt it to the evidence. Slide counts are defaults, not quotas.

## Investor

Use 12–16 slides:

1. Cover — company, category, and one memorable market thesis.
2. Problem — the costly change or broken workflow in customer language.
3. Why now — sourced technological, regulatory, or behavioral inflection.
4. Solution — what becomes possible and for whom.
5. Product flow — trigger, action, and outcome.
6. Value — time, cost, risk, or revenue impact; label modeled values.
7. Market — TAM/SAM/SOM with method, currency, geography, date, and sources.
8. Traction — observed revenue, growth, usage, retention, pilots, or pipeline with periods.
9. Business model — buyer, pricing unit, margin logic, and expansion path.
10. Competition — alternatives and durable differentiation.
11. Go-to-market — wedge, acquisition motion, sales cycle, and expansion.
12. Moat or roadmap — what compounds and what remains to be built.
13. Team — founder-market fit and key hiring gaps.
14. Raise — amount, runway, milestones, and use of funds.

If market or traction numbers are missing, do not fabricate them. Omit the claim, show a labeled measurement plan, or mark the deck as incomplete for external circulation.

## Research

Use 10–14 slides:

1. Cover — paper title, contribution statement, authors, affiliation, date.
2. Research gap — what existing work fails to capture.
3. Artifact at a glance — dataset, model, system, or study dimensions.
4. Contributions — three or four precise technical contributions.
5. Data or source composition — counts, proportions, provenance, exclusions.
6. Method or pipeline — stages, model choices, validation, storage.
7. Variants or conditions — experimental groups, modes, or ablations.
8. Representation or system design — schema, protocol, or architecture.
9. Quality controls — record-level and aggregate checks.
10. Representative example — compact input/output, trajectory, or interface.
11. Evaluation design — metrics, baselines, splits, leakage controls.
12. Results — measured findings with uncertainty; omit if not evaluated.
13. Limitations and risks — bias, coverage, licensing, validity, safety.
14. Takeaways and next experiment — current artifact and next falsifiable milestone.

Keep training, validation, and held-out evaluation assets visually and verbally distinct.

## Product or launch

Use 8–12 slides:

1. Cover — product promise and audience.
2. Old world — painful workflow or constraint.
3. New capability — the product's decisive change.
4. Product moment — one real interface, command, or user action.
5. How it works — three to five steps.
6. Proof — observed outcome, benchmark, case, or customer evidence.
7. Differentiation — why existing alternatives cannot deliver the same outcome.
8. Use cases — distinct users or jobs, not feature categories.
9. Rollout — availability, prerequisites, migration, or adoption path.
10. Closing — one memorable promise and next action.

Do not present roadmap behavior as shipped. Label previews, targets, and planned capabilities.

## Company, brand, or capabilities

Use 8–12 slides:

1. Cover — identity and point of view.
2. Positioning — who the company serves and what it makes possible.
3. Why now — category or customer change.
4. Capabilities — three coherent systems or service pillars.
5. Process — how work moves from discovery to outcome.
6. Evidence — selected projects, products, or measurable results.
7. Differentiation — a direct comparison with common alternatives.
8. Operating model — engagement, pricing, delivery, or collaboration.
9. Team or principles — relevant expertise and standards.
10. Contact or next step.

## Hybrid technical/company

Use when a research artifact is also a product or platform:

1. Cover thesis
2. User or market problem
3. Technical gap
4. System overview
5. Evidence or benchmark
6. Product workflow
7. Architecture or data moat
8. Adoption or traction
9. Market and buyer
10. Business model
11. Risks and governance
12. Roadmap and ask

Do not let commercial framing overstate experimental evidence, or technical detail obscure who benefits and pays.

## Headline test

Read only the slide headlines in order. They should form a coherent argument without body copy. Rewrite headings that merely name a topic—such as “Market,” “Process,” or “Methodology”—into claims supported by the slide.
